## 1. Strategy: Is catalogue-first the right approach?

Verdict: No. A catalogue-led strategy is defensible. A catalogue-first publishing sequence of 25 mandatory Profiles is not.
The plan is organized around Robotist's inventory rather than the decisions its customers need to make.
A university procurement manager does not necessarily begin with "LimX OLI ROS2 Orin NX research platform." They might begin with "humanoid robot for university research," shortlist three platforms, investigate SDK support and maintenance requirements, then search individual model names.
Someone searching "Gausium Phantas price" is further along. Both searches matter, but they require different content.
The fundamental problem
You're treating three different kinds of demand as though they were the same:

| Demand | Example | Best destination |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Model-aware | Gausium Phantas price | Product page or detailed Profile |
| Solution-aware | Best commercial cleaning robot for hotels | Job-specific comparison / Verdict |
| Procurement-ready | Commercial cleaning robot total cost of ownership | Buyer Guide, pricing framework and quote request |

The current plan heavily serves the first category. The other two are where Robotist can demonstrate why a buyer should use a sourcing and deployment partner rather than purchase directly from a manufacturer.
What the live site reveals
I checked robotist.com.au, including the catalogue, comparison page and individual product pages.
There are immediate problems that should be fixed before writing long articles.
1. The publishing plan and live catalogue disagree.
The live site advertises 24 machines. Your plan contains 25, including Qisheng D2, which I could not establish as an existing catalogue entry.

The plan marks Gausium Phantas, Keenon C40-P1, MagicDog Y1 and LimX OLI as quote-only. The live product pages I checked list USD 9,250, USD 10,700, USD 64,900 and USD 65,900 respectively.

3. Some specifications are missing critical conditions.
The Keenon C40-P1 page describes a 15-hour runtime without explaining that Keenon's published specification gives up to 15 hours for sweeping but approximately 5 hours for scrubbing. The Phantas page emphasizes roughly 1,180 m²/hour, while Gausium publishes a practical cleaning efficiency of 350–700 m²/hour.

4. Existing product pages already target model names.
Creating another 1,500-word page for every model risks producing two URLs competing for essentially the same query.
The solution is not necessarily to abandon Profiles. It is to distinguish their purpose:
Product pages: model specifications, price or quote status, availability, warranty and inquiry.
Profiles: fit, limitations, configurations, integration considerations and buying judgment that the product page does not provide.
Verdicts: which machine to select for a particular job, with a defensible evaluation rubric.
Buyer Guides: costs, procurement, operational readiness and purchase risks.
If a Profile adds nothing substantial beyond the product page, expand the product page instead of creating a second URL.

## 2. Queries: What buyers are actually likely to search

Query quality is the weakest part of the current plan.
Many targets read like someone combined a product name, a specification and a buyer persona into a single phrase. That produces something relevant to the article, but not necessarily something anyone searches.
Three recurring mistakes:
Over-specificity: "humanoid robot modular actuators maintenance" combines separate research needs.
Feature-led targeting: "100 TOPS onboard compute" is a specification, not necessarily the buyer's principal search.
Unproven use cases: "care robot elderly assistance" risks implying operational capabilities that need much stronger evidence.
The replacements below are recommended primary search targets, not verified exact-match keywords. I'm approximately 85% confident they better reflect natural buying searches. Actual demand, difficulty and search-result intent still need validation against Search Console, keyword data and sales inquiries.

Three rules for the revised keyword map
Rule 1: Do not confuse an article's subject with its target query.
A Profile about the LimX OLI may reasonably cover NVIDIA computing, ROS 2 support, applications and price. That doesn't make the phrase containing all those terms a good primary keyword.
Rule 2: Keep branded and non-branded demand separate.
"AGIBOT X2 Ultra price" belongs to a model-specific URL.
"Humanoid robots for events" belongs to a solution or comparison URL.
The second query may introduce a qualified buyer who has never heard of AGIBOT.
Rule 3: Don't automatically make the blog the ranking destination.
For searches such as "Gausium Phantas price," I would ordinarily make the existing product URL the canonical transactional destination.
A separate Profile is justified only if it answers a materially different question, such as "Is Gausium Phantas suitable for a hotel with mixed flooring?"
That separation should be confirmed by inspecting actual search results.

## 4. Gaps: What's missing?

Your instincts about comparison, ownership cost, import questions and job-specific content are correct.
But I would rank the missing work by its ability to resolve a purchasing objection.

| Priority | Missing content | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P0 | Job-specific Verdicts | "Which robot works for this facility?" is a buying decision; "which model has more degrees of freedom?" often isn't. |
| P0 | Total cost of ownership | Budget holders need acquisition, setup, consumables, charging, training, support and downtime costs. |
| P0 | Site-readiness checklists | A robot that cannot negotiate doorways, slopes, lifts, flooring or network restrictions is a failed purchase. |
| P0 | Pilot design and acceptance criteria | Defines what success looks like before purchase and reduces disputes over vendor performance claims. |
| P1 | Import, compliance and warranty guides | Especially relevant when purchasing China-sourced robots for Australia or other destinations. |
| P1 | Purchase vs lease vs Robotics-as-a-Service | Converts a capital expenditure problem into a financing and operating-model comparison where these options are actually offered. |
| P1 | University procurement guides | Addresses grants, purchase approval, software licensing, technical documentation, support and spare parts. |
| P1 | Integration and maintenance requirements | Buyers need to know what happens after the robot arrives. |
| P1 | Deployment examples and evidence dossiers | Manufacturer videos prove the robot can be filmed performing a task, not that it works reliably at a customer's site. |
| P2 | Robot buying terminology guide | Useful as a reference for buyers comparing unfamiliar technologies, but not a high-priority lead generator. |

The opportunity I would emphasize most
Practical deployment feasibility.
Robotist's strongest commercial proposition is not access to humanoid hardware. Many companies can publish robot catalogues.
The value is determining whether a particular machine can perform a particular job under real operating constraints, and what the buyer must pay to make it work.
Take a hotel facilities manager evaluating Phantas versus Keenon C40-P1.
The questions that matter include: How much floor area is actually cleanable per shift? How frequently must someone empty wastewater or refill clean water? Can it navigate the relevant doorway widths? What happens around guests? How much manual edge cleaning remains? Who supports the machine when it fails?
These questions offer differentiation without requiring Robotist to claim it has personally tested every model.
Editorial implication: Add a fourth content type, Buyer Guide, around 1,000–1,800 words when justified. It should cover costs, feasibility, procurement and operating requirements. Neither Signals nor Profiles comfortably serve that purpose.

## 5. Standard: Over-engineered or under-specified?

Your standard is stronger on formatting discipline than on buying judgment.
That's fixable.
What is over-engineered

| Current requirement | Problem | Replacement |

| Profile must be 1,200–1,800 words | Creates padding for obscure products with limited verifiable information. | 700–1,800 when warranted. No minimum if the buyer question is answered. |

| Two sibling articles per post | Not practical when the blog currently has no articles listed. |

| Global Robotics Platform | Add related articles as they are published. |

| Mandatory comparison table and four-question FAQ | Makes articles mechanically identical. | Include only when there are real alternatives and useful questions. |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cover plus 2–3 inline figures | Encourages decorative imagery when real deployment images aren't available. | Require useful visuals, not a fixed quantity. |
| No competitor product links | Commercially understandable, but an arbitrary absolute ban weakens transparent comparisons. | Allow external evidence when it materially helps buyers, while prioritizing internal conversion paths. |

What is under-specified
A. Product-claim verification
Every consequential technical statement needs the operating condition attached.
For example, the MagicDog Y1 manufacturer's published specifications distinguish 150 kg maximum payload from 45 kg dynamic payload. Calling it a 45 kg payload robot without context loses information a buyer needs.
MagicLab
Every Profile should separate:
Manufacturer specification
Manufacturer demonstration
Independent reported result
Robotist-verified finding
Unknown or unverified performance
These categories cannot be blended.
B. The meaning of price
You need one pricing data model used by the catalogue, product pages and editorial system.
At minimum: currency, configuration, price type (public list, indicative, dealer quote), date confirmed, freight/tax exclusions, geographic scope and source.
A price shown on a reseller site must not silently become Robotist's confirmed offer. For example, AGIBOT's own store currently presents the X2 Ultra New Version as price-on-request, while another robotics seller advertises USD 49,999. Those are different commercial offers, not necessarily equivalent prices.
Humanoid Robot for Guidance & Development
+1
C. Commercial approval
Your validator checks editorial quality. It also needs to check whether the offer can actually be fulfilled.
Before publication, a sales or operations reviewer must verify:
Is the model currently orderable, and in which configuration?
Can Robotist provide a quote for the targeted destination?
Are lead time, warranty, support and integration claims supportable?
Is the call to action connected to a real response process?
D. Search-intent overlap
Add a hard rule: before creating a new URL, inspect the existing product page and related content.
If both pages answer the same primary question, consolidate them or clearly differentiate their intent.
E. Buyer qualification and conversion
An article about an industrial inspection quadruped needs a different conversion path from one about an educational humanoid.
A better CTA for MagicDog Y1 might be "Request an inspection configuration quote." For a university research article, it might be "Request the research configuration and documentation."
The associated inquiry should collect only the information necessary to progress the sale: application, destination, organization, timeline and relevant technical requirements.
F. Freshness and evidence ownership
Quarterly verification of price and two headline specifications is insufficient for fast-changing robotics configurations.
Use event-triggered reviews when a model is revised, a supplier changes pricing, a firmware capability changes materially, or availability changes. Record who approved the technical and commercial facts.
G. SEO publishing requirements
Explicitly require a unique primary intent, canonical URL, descriptive title, accessible tables, crawlable internal links, relevant structured data and Search Console verification.
Google supports product snippets for applicable product and editorial pages, but the schema must match the actual page and offer. Markup does not guarantee a rich result.
Google for Developers
My change: Keep the tone and sourcing rules. Replace most numerical content quotas with evidence requirements, buyer-decision requirements and factual/commercial approval.

## 6. Risk: What actually kills this plan?

Ranked by expected damage to commercial results
1. A factually inconsistent commercial foundation
This is more urgent than content volume.
If the catalogue and new articles disagree on price, runtime, usable throughput or configuration, the blog can amplify existing inaccuracies.
The Keenon 15-hour runtime issue and Phantas theoretical-versus-practical coverage discrepancy are concrete examples.
Fix: Reconcile catalogue data before publishing. No article should be allowed to introduce an unverified commercial claim.
2. Zero proprietary buying intelligence
The current plan does not appear to incorporate inquiry history, supplier quote experience, sales objections, lost deals or real buyer questions.
That means the editorial team is effectively guessing what prospects care about.
Fix: Before writing, review the last 20 qualified inquiries, if available. Extract model requested, buyer organization, application, geography, budget status, objections and next action.
This should be the actual keyword and content-priority starting point.
3. Thin content disguised as depth
Twenty-five articles of similar length and structure will quickly become repetitive if they primarily rearrange manufacturer specifications.
A thousand carefully edited words can still contain no original information.
Google explicitly warns about producing many substantially unoriginal pages for search manipulation, regardless of whether the content is AI-generated or human-written. This does not mean the proposed Profiles automatically violate policy; it means scale without meaningful additional value is a bad SEO bet.
Google for Developers
+1
Fix: Require every Profile to contain at least three decision-relevant findings not already available on Robotist's product page.
If they cannot be supported, do not publish the standalone Profile.
4. No demonstration or operational evidence
Real product photography is better than generated imagery, but manufacturer photography alone does not prove suitability.
For robotics, especially humanoids, video evidence and configuration details can matter more than long written descriptions.
Fix: Seek manufacturer-authorized task footage, annotated diagrams, real operating-condition documents, integration diagrams and actual deployment evidence where available. Label vendor material accurately.
No need to pretend Robotist tested something it did not.
5. Broad geographic claims with no buying-path specificity
"Shipping worldwide" does not answer what a buyer needs to know about import taxes, documentation, radio compliance, local support or delivery responsibility.
Fix: Make procurement guides destination-specific. Start with Australia unless qualified demand shows another market is more valuable.
6. Quote-only pricing without a useful budgeting answer
Quote-only does not inherently kill demand. Enterprise buyers routinely request customized quotations.
But "contact us for pricing" with no indication of what's included or what influences the quote is weak.
Fix: Give verified budget guidance where possible, disclose known cost drivers and explain what information is required for a useful quote. Do not invent price ranges.
7. Measuring traffic instead of qualified opportunities
A post that brings 20 institutional buyers can be more valuable than one attracting 2,000 enthusiasts.
Fix: Track qualified inquiries by article, sales-accepted leads, quotes issued and resulting opportunity value. Use organic impressions and clicks as diagnostic measures, not the ultimate success metric.
The biggest operational failure mode
You spend two months producing a highly controlled content library, but nobody has verified that the planned articles address the questions your sales team receives.
That is how a good editorial system becomes an expensive publishing machine.

Execution conditions
The 10-week pilot should operate under three rules.
Before Week 1: Reconcile live catalogue prices, specifications and available configurations; review existing qualified inquiries; set up article-level lead tracking. This is prerequisite work, not another publishing week.
During Weeks 1–10: Every article gets an identified sales use, one primary search intent, a distinct destination URL, an appropriate inquiry path and an evidence pack. If the Week 8 comparison cannot be supported with credible configuration information, replace it with an inspection-quadruped requirements guide rather than manufacture a ranking.
After Week 10: Review qualified organic inquiries, sales usage, search impressions, query relevance and quote progression. Do not declare a winning topic based on 10 weeks of rankings alone: new content can take longer to establish meaningful search visibility.
Set a continuation gate: new Profiles are approved only when they have demonstrated sales relevance or defensible search opportunity and enough evidence to offer something beyond the catalogue.
Final decision
Do not spend two months writing 25 Profiles.
Preserve the model research inventory, the editorial validator and the human approval process. Change what qualifies an article for publication.
Robotist does not need to become the internet's twenty-fifth source of manufacturer specifications.
It needs to become a credible place for a buyer to determine:
Which robot fits my operation, what will it actually cost to get working, and what could go wrong?
That's the content worth building.
